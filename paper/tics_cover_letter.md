# Cover Letter — Trends in Cognitive Sciences Submission

---

Dear Editor-in-Chief,

We write to submit our Review article, **"Pitch–Hue Correspondences Across Three Centuries: A Quantitative Synthesis and Theory-Derived Congruency Criterion for Neural Research,"** for consideration in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*.

Cross-modal correspondence — the tendency for stimuli across sensory modalities to feel like they "belong" together — is among the most actively debated problems in perception research, yet the field lacks principled criteria for selecting the right dimensions when designing experiments. Our Review addresses that gap directly. We assembled a corpus of 70 pitch–hue mapping systems spanning three centuries and eight reasoning traditions, from Newton's prism analogy through contemporary music education, and subjected them to a unified quantitative analysis. The headline result is counterintuitive but theoretically important: global agreement across systems is near chance (mean r = 0.221, simulated chance = 0.212), yet hue assignments are non-uniform (Rayleigh R = .66, p = .003), and when cross-modal dimensions are aligned in affective space the match is significant (p = .027). The dominant organizing axis is Evaluation — the good–bad dimension of emotional valence — not the brightness or arousal dimensions that most neural work has implicitly assumed.

This finding is the right kind of synthesis for *TICS*. The journal's core function is to take a contested body of evidence and extract the result that changes how the field frames its next experiments; that is precisely what this Review delivers. Cognitive scientists working in multisensory perception, affective neuroscience, and auditory or visual processing will immediately recognize the Evaluation-axis result as resolving a three-century deadlock that persisted because no previous account treated the full system landscape as data. Our Box 2 translates this directly into actionable congruency criteria for EEG and fMRI paradigms, giving the readership a ready-made design tool rather than just a theoretical claim.

The scope is appropriately cross-cutting for a TICS audience: the synthesis touches perception, emotional coding, cross-modal learning, and the neural substrates of affective meaning — all central to the journal's readership.

We confirm that this manuscript has not been published previously and is not under consideration elsewhere. No preprint has been posted. All authors have read and approved the submission. We declare no conflicts of interest and no funding to disclose.
